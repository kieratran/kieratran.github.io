My Projects

Radar-derived basal melt rates of Ross Ice Shelf, Antractica

This project examines the relationship between airborne ice-penetrating radar attenuation rates and ice-shelf basal melt rates through ice temperature at Ross Ice Shelf (RIS). We build a 1.5-D thermal model to capture this direct relationship and convert melt rates from depth-resolved multiple-reflector attenuation rates. Radar data was from ROSETTA-Ice airborne survey. We discover higher basal melt rates at grounding zones of both eastern and western RIS, which reveal complex mixtures of melting processes caused by tides, ocean currents, subglacial hydrology discharge, and basal topography. This work is currently in prep.

Evolution of ice-shelf basal channels at Pine Island shear margin

This is a NASA FINESST project with collaboration with Dr. Karen Alley and her students (Adam Isaak & Dylan Kreynen), who develop an automated algorithm to detect surface depression of ice-shelf basal channels. Simultaneously, I run swath analysis to obtain 3-D images of basal topography to observe crevasse formation at the channel apex. Together, we investigate the evolution of basal channels that links with ice-shelf collapse at PIG.

Understanding hydrostatic equilibrium through physics-informed machine learning model

I am also interested in understanding hydrostatic imbalance near the grounding zone, where the assumptions of hydrostatic equilibrium become unreliable. I plan to develop a physics-informed machine learning model trained on the relationship between surface and basal elevation observed with ice-penetrating radar. This model could be used to infer plausible basal elevations across Antarctic ice shelves, particularly in regions where direct observations are limited. Ultimately, this approach could help us better understand basal geometry and the transition from grounded to floating ice.

Alissa Ferguson - Monitoring Kīlauea Volcano using InSAR

Click here to watch full presentation.

Read abstract

Persistent eruptive volcanoes, such as Kīlauea, can deform rapidly and erupt multiple times per month, causing lateral damage to surrounding areas. This movement has been monitored continuously using on-site Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) stations, complemented by repeated satellite observations, to investigate internal changes of Kīlauea’s complex magma chamber. Due to its activeness, frequent and fast data processes for emergency responses are necessary and need to be well-established. In this study, we utilize the accessibility of Sentinel-1A Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and GNSS stations while creating a working pipeline for frequent monitoring of the deformation Kīlauea. First, surface displacement rates are calculated by the Small Baseline Subset (SBAS) inversion approach, which solves for an instantaneous velocity at each SAR acquisition time between October 1, 2025 and February 1, 2026 and integrates these velocities. Then, three locations are extracted corresponding to GNSS stations for direct comparison, including one control site (~12km from summit) and two sites each within a kilometer of the summit. The results are within the expected range of surface deformation, which show possible implications for consistent and updated observations. Furthermore, interferograms and coherence maps are computed to examine the changes related to displacement rate. However, C-Band Sentinel-1A based maps are noisy because of significant vegetation density. In the future, this pipeline could potentially apply to NISAR to reduce vegetation noise and, when used in conjunction with other SAR satellites, provide more measurements for better monitoring of Kīlauea and other active volcanoes.

Catherine Pope - Impacts of glacial lake outburst flood on Alaskan wetland

Click here to watch full presentation.

Read abstract

Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs), catastrophic meltwater release from a lake fed by a glacier, are becoming more frequent and severe worldwide, posing increasing risks to downstream communities and ecosystems. In Juneau, Alaska, annual GLOFs from Suicide Basin have reached major flood stage since 2023, inundating portions of the Mendenhall Wetlands State Game and Refuge. Despite increasing impacts to infrastructure, the ecological responses of the downstream wetland remains poorly understood. This study develops a multi-year remote sensing framework to evaluate vegetation dynamics in response to repeated GLOFs. We use Harmonized Landsat-Sentinel-2 (HLS) imagery to generate time series of Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI - the health, density, and "greenness" of vegetation) and Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI - the present and content of liquid water) over three high marsh study areas, including two potentially flood-impacted marshes and one reference marsh. Preliminary results indicate consistent seasonal vegetation change in all marsh areas, with NDVI peaking during midsummer and declining through fall, which correlates with meltwater outbursts in flooding years. However, these mean values alone cannot conclusively distinguish NDVI signals from seasonal variation and impacts of major GLOF events. Therefore, flood-related changes may be more subtle than changes in average greenness. Ongoing work is expanding the analysis to object-based landscape metrics to investigate changes in vegetation structure and fragmentation that may not be captured by traditional vegetation indices. This work will improve understanding of GLOFs impacts on downstream ecosystems and support wetland management and habitat conservation in Southeast Alaska.

Charlie Rosemond - Wildfire mapping of the Dragon Bravo Fire using UAVSAR

Click here to watch full presentation.

Read abstract

As droughts in the American West worsen, wildfires become more unpredictable. These fires can burn for months, threatening critical ecosystems and infrastructure. Researchers frequently utilize remote sensing to map the progression and spread of wildfires. However, issues arise from low temporal resolution, limited spatial coverage, and disruptions due to cloud cover or smoke. The newly launched NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) could help address these issues by enabling researchers to map burn scars with higher temporal resolution and greater spatial coverage. These data-processing pipelines have already been created for Uncrewed Aerial Vehicle Synthetic Aperture Radar (UAVSAR) burn-scar mapping. This research uses airborne L-Band UAVSAR data to examine how backscatter variations can be used to classify burn severity across different stages of wildfire progression. The L-Band backscatter changes were categorized into four burn-severity classes and compared with Landsat-8/9-based burn-severity classifications, with the UAVSAR-derived highly burned area classification label being 8.8 times that of Landsat. The UAVSAR-based data processing workflow is now being applied to simulated NISAR data to assess how well airborne SAR methods scale to spaceborne wildfire mapping. These results show that while L-Band SAR classifies burn severity to a greater spatial extent than Landsat, it is capable of mapping wildfire burn progression. With NISAR's improved temporal resolution and spatial coverage, wildfire incident commanders will be better informed when making emergency management decisions, and researchers will gain a better understanding of wildfire progression.

Ella Boussy - Biomass differences between protected and unprotected areas of Cheasepeak Bay

Click here to watch full presentation.

Read abstract

The Chesapeake Bay Watershed is the largest and most productive estuary in the United States, supporting 41 million acres of forests, wetlands, rivers, and streams. Protected lands within the watershed play an important role in maintaining ecosystem health and supporting ecological functions. Previous studies have assessed aboveground biomass density (AGBD) across a variety of forest ecosystems, regions, and land-use types; however, direct comparisons of AGBD between protected and unprotected lands remain limited. To investigate this matter, protected and unprotected land data from the United States Geological Survey’s Protected Area Database (USGS PAD) were overlaid with AGBD derived from NASA’s Global Ecosystem Dynamics Lidar (GEDI) L4B mission. We compare biomass between these areas using a k-dimensional tree algorithm to identify the nearest unprotected pixel within 10 km of each protected pixel. The results show that mean biomass was higher in matched protected areas (97.91 Mg/ha) than in unprotected counterparts (86.31 Mg/ha), yielding a difference of +11.60 Mg/ha (+13.4%). Protected areas had a statistically significant higher in aboveground biomass compared to unprotected areas; however, the observed effect was smaller than the average uncertainty of the GEDI biomass estimates. Additional validation with an independent biomass dataset would strengthen confidence in this finding. These values support the efforts in conservation and management of forest areas, which further help maintain the health of surrounding ecosystems.

Jackson Retelle - Detecting landslides caused by hurricane Helene using SAR

Click here to watch full presentation.

Read abstract

Hurricane Helene (2024) was a category 4 hurricane that caused widespread damage in the Southern Appalachians. Hurricane Helene was responsible for over 250 deaths, $78 billion dollars in damages (National Hurricane Center, 2026), and more than 2,000 landslides (United States Geological Survey, 2025). Optical remote sensing is a well-established tool for assessing landslides; however, its effectiveness is severely mitigated by visual obscurations such as clouds. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is a promising technology that allows for landslide detection through visual obscurations. This study examines the efficacy of SAR backscatter change detection in identifying landslides in the wake of Hurricane Helene (2024). We utilized a Google Earth Engine script to conduct time series analysis of Sentinel-1 imagery. The change in the median backscatter coefficient is analyzed to identify potential landslide locations, which are then validated against known landslides. The results show a mix of landslides, mudslides, flooding, and other damage. While known landslides are properly identified, other backscatter changes caused by wind and flooding damage confound the results. Understanding the limitations of SAR imagery and how to accurately assess landslides could improve situational awareness for decision makers during future events.

Morgan Fellows - Tracking tornadoes in mid-west USA using SAR backscatter

Click here to watch full presentation.

Read abstract

Major tornado outbreaks, consisting of at least six tornadoes spawned from the same weather system in less than 24 hours, require accurate damage assessments to support hazard analysis and improve understanding of tornado impacts. Current damage assessments rely on radar observations, eyewitness accounts, and documented damage, while the potential of Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) to provide observations in all weather conditions remains underutilized. This study explores the use of Sentinel-1A SAR intensity data in identifying tornado tracks associated with the March 14, 2025 tornado outbreak in the Midwestern United States. Out of the twelve tornadoes analyzed within the St. Louis County warning area, two tornado tracks were successfully identified using differences in backscatter signals. These tornadoes were the highest-rated events (EF-3) and occurred in areas of limited human habitation. The remaining ten tornadoes, which were EF-2 or weaker and primarily located in more populated regions, were not clearly identifiable from the SAR data. This result demonstrates the potential for SAR to complement traditional tornado damage assessment methods by providing observations regardless of cloud cover or weather conditions. Incorporating SAR into post-event assessments could improve damage characterization, particularly in areas where traditional observations are limited.
